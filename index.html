Ladder Safety – set up right, climb smart

About 15 minutes

Before you start

Ladders are on almost every job we do, so they're easy to stop thinking about. This toolbox walks through choosing, setting up, using and looking after a ladder, plus fatigue and what to do if someone falls.

1 · Why it matters

It's the short falls that get people

Most of us picture a fall from a roof edge. In reality, most serious fall injuries in construction happen from under 3 metres – the height of a single-storey gutter – and ladders are a big part of that.

83%of serious fall injuries in construction happen below 3 m
40%of serious injuries from below 2 m involve a ladder
Halfof fatal falls from roofs and ladders were from less than 4 m

Sources: SafeWork SA falls data (construction); Safe Work Australia Construction Industry Profile.

What a "small" fall can actually cost

  • Your body: broken wrists, ribs and ankles, deep cuts, head injuries and back or spinal damage – all common from a 2–3 m fall onto concrete, a retaining wall or a step.
  • Your time: weeks or months off the tools. Not driving, not lifting, not sleeping well.
  • Your work: lost income and overtime, time off the tools, and a slow road back to full duties.
  • Home life: not being able to pick up your kids, play sport, or get through a normal day without pain.
The good news: almost every ladder fall comes down to a handful of things you control – the ladder you pick, how you set it up, how you use it, and how you're feeling. That's what the next pages cover.
2 · Pick the right ladder

Is a ladder the right tool?

Ladders are for short, light work where you can keep three points of contact. If the job needs both hands, pushing or pulling, lifting something heavy, or a long time up there, look at a platform ladder, mobile scaffold or EWP instead. Talk with your Service Manager if you don't have the right gear.

Check the load rating – it's you plus everything you carry

Know your number. Read the rating label before you climb – if you can't read it, don't use the ladder.
  • Only use industrial rated ladders (AS/NZS 1892). Domestic ladders from home stay at home.
  • Industrial ladders are usually rated 120 kg or 150 kg. The rating includes your body weight, clothes, tool belt and anything you carry up.
  • Near anything electrical, use a non-conductive (fibreglass) ladder – never aluminium.
  • Pick a ladder long enough that you don't have to stand near the top.

Dual purpose (step/extension) ladders

  • Hinges and locks must be fully clicked in before you load it – check, don't assume.
  • As a lean-to ladder it's shorter and lighter-duty than a full extension ladder, so it's easy to end up using it too steep, too short, or standing too high. If it barely reaches, get a longer ladder.
  • Follow the angle and use markings on the side of the ladder.
3 · Set it up right

Base, angle, top, tie

The 4:1 rule – 1 metre out for every 4 metres up – with at least 1 metre of ladder above the gutter.

1. The base

✓ Firm, flat, level ground with both feet fully on it. Use proper ladder levellers on uneven ground.
✗ Don't stand the foot on a retaining wall, garden edge, step, paver lip, soft lawn or wet tiles. Don't pack it up with bricks or timber offcuts. If the base isn't right, move the ladder or change the setup.

2. The angle

  • Use the 4:1 rule – about 1 m out for every 4 m of height.
  • Quick check: stand with your toes at the feet of the ladder and arms straight out – your palms should just touch a rung.
  • Too steep and it can tip backwards. Too flat and the feet can kick out.

3. The top

Houses don't have a dedicated spot for a ladder, so most of the time the top ends up on the wall or the gutter. Check it before you lean on it.

✗ Gutters aren't built to carry a ladder and a person. Old, rusted, plastic, sagging or loose gutters can bend or buckle, and the ladder moves with them.
✓ Before you climb, check the top:
  • Working below the eaves? Rest it on the wall instead of the gutter.
  • If it has to go on the gutter, make sure it's metal, sound and firmly fixed. Sit the stiles at or right beside a gutter bracket, or near a corner where it's strongest.
  • Both stiles in full contact, and the ladder can't slide sideways along the gutter.
  • Keep your weight centred – don't push, pull or lever against the gutter while you work.
Gutter looks dodgy? Don't rest on it or secure to it. Change the setup – move to a better spot or use a platform or step ladder. If none of that works, call your Service Manager before you climb.
  • The ladder must extend at least 1 m above the gutter (or wherever you get on and off). That gives you something to hold onto as you step on or off.

4. Secure the top

The top of the ladder must be secured to something sturdy before you work from it.

  • Lockjaw ladder grip (if you have one): hook it on the stile, clamp it under the lip of a sound metal gutter and turn to lock. Not for plastic gutters. Check it's locked before you climb.
  • No grip? Ratchet strap the stiles (not the rungs) to something sturdy. The gutter is the last choice – only if it's metal, sound and firmly fixed, and strap at a bracket.
  • Can't secure the top? Have someone foot the ladder and keep it to short, light work – or use a platform ladder or other access instead.
  • Secure the base too – good anti-slip feet, or tie or stake it where you can.
  • On extension ladders, check the locks are engaged and the sections overlap properly.
4 · Using it

Three points, centred, not too high

  • Face the ladder going up and down. Hands on the stiles or rungs, not full of gear.
  • Carry tools in a belt or pouch. Lift parts and heavier gear up with a rope/hoist, or pass them up – don't climb one-handed with them.
  • One person on the ladder at a time.
  • No pushing, pulling or levering hard from a ladder – removing old fittings, brackets or units can throw you off balance when something lets go.
  • Check overhead for powerlines and service mains before you raise or move a ladder. Lower it to move it.
  • Don't climb in strong wind or rain, or on wet/muddy rungs and boots.
  • Keep the area at the base clear and, where people walk past, set up a barrier or cone.
Plumbing: hot water and solar jobs often mean lifting fittings, pipe and old components at roof height. Plan how gear gets up and down before you climb – rope, a second person, or a platform – and never carry it in your hands up the ladder.
Electrical: fibreglass only. Watch for service mains and overhead lines when raising or walking a ladder, and don't rest a ladder against a point of attachment or consumer mains.
HVAC: outdoor units on walls and roofs are heavy and awkward. If the lift or the job needs both hands, a ladder isn't the right platform – talk with your Service Manager about a platform ladder, scaffold or second person.
Carpentry / Handyman: fascia, gutter and eave work tempts you to lean out. Move the ladder rather than reach, and never rest it on the gutter you're working on.
Data / Security / Solar: fibreglass ladders around any electrical work, cameras and antennas on fascias mean overreaching – move the ladder, don't stretch.
5 · Look after it

30-second check before every climb

  • Stiles: no bends, dents, cracks or splits. Fibreglass shouldn't look chalky or have fibres showing (sun damage).
  • Rungs/steps: tight, not bent, clean – no grease, mud or silicone.
  • Feet: all there, rubber not worn through, swivels working.
  • Locks, hinges and spreaders: engage fully and click in; no loose rivets.
  • Labels: rating and use labels readable.
  • Ropes/pulleys (extension ladders): not frayed, pulley turns freely.
Found a problem? Don't use it. Tag it "DO NOT USE", take it out of service and tell your Service Manager so it gets repaired or replaced. Never "make do" with a damaged ladder, and never patch one up yourself.

On the van

  • Strap ladders down properly on the rack – loose ladders get bent and shaken apart.
  • Don't stack heavy gear on top of them.
  • Keep them clean and dry; wash off mud, sealant and concrete.
6 · Fatigue and heat

Tired legs, tired decisions

Ladders need balance, grip and attention. All three drop off when you're tired, hot or dehydrated – and Brisbane summers make that worse.

  • Long days, early starts, after-hours callouts and back-to-back jobs all add up.
  • Late in the day is when people rush to get finished – that's exactly when shortcuts get taken on setup.
  • Heat and dehydration cause dizziness, sweaty hands and slower reactions.
Before you climb, ask yourself: Am I rushing? Am I drained or light-headed? Have I had water? If the answer worries you – stop, drink, take five, and set up properly. If it's late and the job can't be done safely, call your Service Manager about finishing it the next day or getting a second person.
Look out for your workmates too. If someone on the job looks cooked or is taking shortcuts with a ladder, say something – whoever they are.
7 · If someone falls

Get help, get checked, report it

If someone else falls

  • Make the area safe, then check on them. If they hit their head, neck or back, don't move them unless they're in danger – keep them still.
  • Call 000 for anything more than a minor knock, then call your Service Manager.
  • Give first aid within your training (control bleeding, keep them warm and talking).

If you fall

Adrenaline hides injuries. Broken ribs, internal injuries and back damage often don't feel serious straight away. Feeling "okay" isn't the same as being okay.
  • Stop work. Call your Service Manager straight away.
  • Get checked by a doctor or hospital – and don't drive yourself. Have someone take you, or call an ambulance.
  • Report it, even if you feel fine. Every fall and near miss gets reported – it's how we fix the cause for everyone.
Nobody gets in trouble for reporting a fall or near miss. Not reporting is what stops us fixing it.
